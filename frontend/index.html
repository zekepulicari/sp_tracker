    Correlation (Pearson & Spearman)

    Exploratory Analysis
    • What it is: Correlation measures whether two variables change together. For example, does the frequency of species sightings increase when sea surface temperature rises?
    • Pearson Correlation: Measures linear relationships, essentially checking if the two variables can be described by a straight line.
    • Spearman's Rank Correlation: Measures monotonic relationships. This is useful for identifying general upward or downward trends, even if the relationship is not perfectly linear (e.g., a curve).
    • Output: Both methods provide a correlation coefficient (showing the strength and direction of the relationship) and a p-value (indicating statistical significance).
    Learn more about Correlation ↗

    Multicollinearity (VIF)

    Data Quality Check
    • What it is: Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) is a diagnostic tool used to detect multicollinearity. This occurs when two or more predictor variables (like Air Temperature and Sea Surface Temperature) are highly correlated with each other.
    • Why it matters: If variables are too similar, they provide redundant information, which can make statistical models unstable and their coefficients difficult to interpret.
    • How to interpret: VIF quantifies how much the variance of a regression coefficient is inflated due to this redundancy. A VIF value above 5 or 10 typically indicates problematic multicollinearity, suggesting that one of the redundant variables should be removed.
    Learn more about VIF ↗

    Generalized Linear Model (GLM)

    Binary Classification
    • What it is: In this application, we use a specific type of GLM known as Logistic Regression. It calculates the probability of a binary outcome—in our case, the presence (1) or absence (0) of a species.
    • How it works: The model takes environmental conditions (such as water temperature and current velocity) at locations where the species was observed, and compares them to randomly generated "pseudo-absence" points.
    • Output: It fits a logistic curve to these data points, allowing us to predict the probability of encountering the species given a specific set of environmental conditions.
    Learn more about GLM ↗

    Random Forest

    Machine Learning / Ensemble
    • What it is: Random Forest is a powerful machine learning algorithm that creates an "ensemble" of many individual decision trees. Each tree is trained on a random subset of the data and a random subset of the predictor variables.
    • How it works: When making a prediction, the Random Forest aggregates the "votes" from all of its constituent trees. This makes it highly robust against overfitting.
    • Advantages: It is exceptionally capable of capturing complex, non-linear interactions between environmental variables (for instance, if high wind speeds only matter when the water is warm).
    • Feature Importance: It also provides a measure of "feature importance," revealing which environmental factors are most influential in predicting species presence.
    Learn more about Random Forest ↗

    Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA)

    Multivariate Ecology
    • What it is: Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) is a multivariate ordination technique frequently used in community ecology. Instead of just predicting presence or absence, CCA relates species composition directly to environmental gradients.
    • How it works: It projects the species occurrence data onto axes that are constrained to be linear combinations of the environmental variables.
    • Interpretation: By doing so, CCA visually and statistically highlights the specific environmental "niche" or preferences of the species, showing how different environmental factors structure the biological community.

    Purpose

    This begins with a story. I took a plane to see orcas for the first time. I spent hours sitting on a beach, constantly drinking hot water so I wouldn't freeze in the low temperatures. But days passed, and I saw nothing, even though I had taken the tides and the time of year into account based on professional guidance.

    That was my motivation to create this project. I needed the power of statistics and machine learning, fed by historical observations and additional variables. I knew that other factors were important, like the wind, which I hadn't been able to take into account at the time.
